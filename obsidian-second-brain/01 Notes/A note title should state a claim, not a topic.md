# A note title should state a claim, not a topic

> [!abstract] The claim
> A title that asserts something makes the note's links readable as arguments; a
> title that names a subject makes them readable as filing labels.

## Why it holds

Compare these two sentences as they'd appear inside another note:

- "This is a specific case of [[AI tools]]." — tells you nothing.
- "This is a specific case of [[AI collapses the cost of the first draft, not the last]]." — tells you the whole argument.

The link text *is* the idea, so the network becomes readable prose instead of a
card catalogue. You can follow a chain of five links and end up with a coherent
line of thinking rather than five folders.

Claim-titles also enforce atomicity as a side effect. If you can't state the note
in one declarative sentence, you don't have one note — you have two, and writing
the title is what reveals it.

## Where it breaks

Reference material genuinely is topical. A note holding a client's brand
guidelines, a list of keyboard shortcuts, or a person's contact details has no
claim to make, and forcing one is precious. Those live in `03 Sources`,
`05 People`, or a Map of Content — not in `01 Notes`.

Claim-titles are also worse for *searching* when you can't remember the wording.
That's what [[Links carry meaning that folders cannot|links and MOCs]] are for:
you navigate to a claim-note by association, not by recall.
